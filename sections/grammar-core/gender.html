Gender

Every noun is masculine or feminine.
Articles (el / la), adjectives, and many pronouns must match that gender (and singular/plural).
Possessive forms like el mío / la mía only make sense once you know gender.

Feminine plural las lahs the las casas lahs KAH-sahs

  • el libro ehl LEE-broh
    the book
  • la casa lah KAH-sah
    the house
  • el perro ehl PEH-rroh
    the dog
  • la mesa lah MEH-sah
    the table
  • los libros lohs LEE-brohs
    the books
  • las casas lahs KAH-sahs
    the houses
  • el niño ehl NEE-nyoh
    the boy
  • la niña lah NEE-nyah
    the girl
  • los niños lohs NEE-nyohs
    the boys / the children (mixed)
  • las niñas lahs NEE-nyahs

    the hand (feminine noun)

Tip: Feminine nouns that begin with a stressed a- sound take el in the singular to avoid the awkward "la a" sound: el agua ehl AH-gwah, el águila ehl AH-gee-lah. They are still feminine — use feminine adjectives: el agua fría ehl AH-gwah FREE-ah.


The indefinite article — "a / an / some"
Gender / number Spanish Pronunciation English Used with Pronunciation
Masculine singular un oon a / an un libro oon LEE-broh
Feminine singular una OO-nah a / an una casa OO-nah KAH-sah
Masculine plural unos OO-nohs some unos libros OO-nohs LEE-brohs
Feminine plural unas OO-nahs some unas casas OO-nahs KAH-sahs
Example sentences:
  • un libro oon LEE-broh
    a book
  • una casa OO-nah KAH-sah
    a house
  • un amigo oon ah-MEE-goh
    a (male) friend
  • una amiga OO-nah ah-MEE-gah
    a (female) friend
  • unos perros OO-nohs PEH-rrohs
    some dogs
  • unas flores OO-nahs FLOH-rehs
    some flowers
  • un problema oon proh-BLEH-mah
    a problem (masculine noun)
  • una mano OO-nah MAH-noh
    a hand (feminine noun)
  • Quiero un café. KYEH-roh oon kah-FEH
    I want a coffee.
  • Necesito una pluma. neh-seh-SEE-toh OO-nah PLOO-mah
    I need a pen.

Tip: Un and una are also used as the number one.
Unlike English, Spanish uses the indefinite article even with professions: Soy un estudiante. soy oon ehs-too-DYAHN-teh (I'm a student).

Why el agua?
  • Feminine words that start with a stressed a- / ha- sound take el in the singular (easier to say), but stay feminine:
  • el agua fría ehl AH-gwah FREE-ah
    the cold water (fría is feminine)
  • las aguas lahs AH-gwahs
    the waters (plural back to las)
  • el águila calva ehl AH-gee-lah KAHL-bah
    the bald eagle (calva is feminine)

Key patterns:
  • Adjectives agree in gender (masculine/feminine) and number (singular/plural) with the noun they describe.
  • Masculine singular: alto AHL-toh
  • Feminine singular: alta AHL-tah
  • Masculine plural: altos AHL-tohs
  • Feminine plural: altas AHL-tahs
  • Adjectives ending in -e or a consonant (like interesante, grande) don't change for gender — only number: interesantes een-teh-reh-SAHN-tehs.

Pronoun tips
  • tú (you) has an accent; tu (your) does not.
  • él (he) has an accent; el (the) does not.
  • mí (me, after preposition) has an accent; mi (my) does not.
  • Subject pronouns are optional: Hablo español already means "I speak Spanish."
  • LatAm: everyday plural "you" = ustedes (not vosotros).
  • Spain: informal plural "you" = vosotros; formal plural = ustedes.
  • su / suyo is ambiguous (his/her/your/their) — Spanish often clarifies with de él, de ella, de usted , etc.

Gender tips
  • Learn nouns with the article: el libro, la casa — not just libro / casa.
  • -o often masculine, -a often feminine — but watch exceptions (el día, la mano, el problema).
  • Plural mixed groups usually use masculine: los niños, los amigos.
  • Adjectives match the noun: casa blanca, libro rojo.
  • Speaker gender shows up in soy alto/alta, estoy cansado/cansada, encantado/encantada.
  • Possessives like mío/mía match the owned thing, not the owner.
  • el agua, el águila: singular el, but adjective stays feminine (agua fría).
